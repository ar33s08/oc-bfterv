The whole offense in one table

Seven defenders is a fixed number. Every front is just them choosing where to be short. The job is never to beat their best player — it's to attack the spot they left empty. This is the entire policy; the plays are how you execute each row.

The counting rule

Offense has seven too. Subtract their rushers from seven and that's how many bodies they have in coverage. You have six eligibles plus a running QB. So:

  • They rush 1 → six in coverage on six eligibles plus a QB. Nobody is on the QB. That's the biggest edge in this league.
  • They rush 2 → five in coverage. You have a plus-one somewhere, every snap.
  • They rush 3 → four in coverage. Six eligibles on four bodies: somebody is uncovered at the snap.
  • They rush 4+ → three or fewer in coverage and four free lanes. Quick game, screens, QB takeoff.
  • They rush 0 → all seven have their backs turned and nobody can tackle a running QB.

Free rusher, no real blocking, so the clock is about 2.2 seconds. Every play here gets the ball out, or the QB past the line, before that. Anything needing longer buys the time with a chip first.

Install rules

Non-negotiable. These are what make the plays on the other tabs actually work.

0. The center is the engine of this offense

He snaps every single play, and he is the only player who is never covered at the snap. Seven defenders have their eyes on the receivers and the QB — nobody is assigned to the snapper. That makes him two weapons at once:

  • As a blocker: he's already at the ball, so he's the only player who can touch the interior rusher without giving ground. Hands behind the back, body in the lane, one beat, then release.
  • As a receiver: when he snaps and releases clean, he is wide open against any front that rushes two or fewer. That's a free 7 yards, and against man it's a walk-in because his defender is in trail on somebody else.

Rules for the center, every rep:

  1. Snap, then head up in under half a second. If he's slow out of the snap he's a statue, and the rush beats him to the QB.
  2. Blocking: never chase past the line. Get in the lane, take one beat, release. If you chase, you block nobody and run nobody open.
  3. Releasing: snap and go. No fake, no hesitation — a clean release is worth more than a half-hearted chip.
  4. He is always live. Any play with the center releasing, the QB's checkdown is the center at 6 yards. Take it when nothing else is there.

Every play in the book already tells the center his job — snap and set, snap and lead, chip then flat, snap and release. That's the C row in every assignment table, and his route is drawn in amber.

1. The chip is a speed bump, not a wall

Hands behind your back means no punch, no lockout, no anchor. Stop trying to hold anybody. The chip's only job is to put your body in the rusher's path for one beat.

  • Line up in his lane, not next to him. Speeds off the edge: get in the runway.
  • Step into him and turn your shoulder in — a moving pick, not a blocker.
  • Never chase. Once he's past your hip, release into the route.
  • The chip is a free release into the flat. When they rush hard, the chipper in the flat is usually the openest man on the field.

2. Kais and Fareed both run the whole book

Same playbook, same formations, same motions — the defense never gets to know which one is in. Both can run the QB series and both can throw it over the top. Only the flavor changes:

  • Kais in: lean on QB Power, Jet Keep, Empty QB Iso, Zone Keep. Every time he runs it, a defender has to stop looking at a receiver — so the next snap somebody is open by three steps.
  • Fareed in: lean on Four Verts, Deep Cross, Post-Wheel, Play-Action Bomb. Two-deep safeties are two guys who can't help on a crosser: make them choose, throw behind the choice.
  • Dual: put them on the field together and use motion and throwback. Jet motion with a throwback, or orbit motion with the QB leaking — with either one live as the runner and the other live as the thrower. Defenses never match it up correctly.
  • Never signal which one is at QB. If they guess wrong they're in the wrong coverage all drive.

3. Scramble drill — a rule, not a play

The moment the QB breaks contain, four things happen, every time, no signal:

  1. Nearest eligible comes back to the QB at 8-10 yards. Bail-out.
  2. The receiver on the QB's throwing-hand side takes his route deep.
  3. The receiver on the opposite side crosses the field behind the QB's path. In flag that's a 30-yard play.
  4. Everyone else gets to a window and stays put. Don't drift into the QB's eyes.

The center leads this: he's the closest body, so he is always the check-back. Five minutes every practice until it's reflex.

4. Motion rules

  • Motion is a read, not decoration. Man travels with the motion; zone doesn't. Call the answer off the motion.
  • Motion before the snap is free — use it to flip a 2x2 into a 3x1 and back.
  • Motion a man into a chip lane when the rusher is winning. A body arriving on the move beats one standing still.
  • Never motion a man across and throw it back unless you've repped the throwback.

5. Coverage keys

  • Eyes: man defenders look at you; zone defenders look at the QB.
  • Depth: tight and up = man/press. Bodies at 8-10 with heads on the QB = zone.
  • Motion: man travels, zone doesn't. Motion twice if you're not sure — some teams travel as a bluff.
  • Count the deep: one safety = post behind the crosser. Two safeties = seam and over, and run the QB at them.

If they disguise well, call the two-play: quick game to the side they show plus a vertical tagged on it. Whichever way they jump, one of the two is right.

6. Cadence

  • Vary the count. The rush can leave on the ball, so a hard count gets you a free five and gets him guessing.
  • Obvious passing downs: snap on 1. Don't let them time it.
  • Red zone: snap on 1 every time. No thinking, no pre-snap penalties.

7. First five to install

Bubble Now, Slant-Flat, Post-Wheel, Four Verts, QB Power — plus Center Leak, because it's free. That is a complete offense; everything else is a variation on one of those six.
