The whole offense in one table

Seven defenders is a fixed number. Every front is just them choosing where to be short. Your job is not to beat their best players — it's to attack the spot they left empty. This is the entire policy; the plays are just how you execute each row.

The counting rule

Offense has 7 too. Subtract their rushers from 7 and that's how many they have in coverage. You have 6 eligibles plus Kai's legs. So:

  • They rush 1 → 6 in coverage vs your 6 eligibles + a running QB. Nobody is on Kai. That is the single biggest edge in this league and it's why 1-5-1 is a gift.
  • They rush 2 → 5 in coverage. You have +1 somewhere, always, plus the QB. Attack the free man, not the middle.
  • They rush 3 → 4 in coverage. Six eligibles vs four bodies: somebody is running wide open every snap. Get it out fast and let them tackle air.
  • They rush 4+ → 3 or fewer in coverage and 2 free rushers. Nothing but quick game, screens, and Kai taking off.

With a free rusher and no real blocking, the clock is ~2.2 seconds. Every play in this book is built so the ball is out, or the QB is past the line, before that clock runs out. If a concept needs 4 seconds to develop, it needs a chip first.

Install rules

Non-negotiable. These are what make the plays on the other tabs actually work. Without them the book is just drawings.

1. The chip is a speed bump, not a wall

Hands behind your back means you have no punch, no lockout, no anchor. So stop trying to hold anybody. The chip has one job: put your body in the rusher's path for one beat and slow him by half a second.

  • Line up in his lane, not next to him. If he's a speed guy off the edge, you are in his runway.
  • Take a step into him and turn your shoulder in — you're a moving pick, not a blocker.
  • Never chase. Once he's past your hip, release into your route. If you chase, you're a coverage defender's best friend.
  • If he's slower than you: get in front, then release late. If he's faster: take him once early and go, the QB is already moving.
  • Chip and release is a free release into the flat. When the defense rushes hard, the chipper running to the flat is usually the openest man on the field.

2. Kai and Fareed are not interchangable — install them separately

Same playbook, two different stress tests, and the threat of one sets up the other.

  • Kai series (1-5-1, 0-6-1, anything with one rusher): QB power, QB draw, jet keep, empty iso. Every snap you run him, a defender has to stop looking at a receiver — which means the next snap somebody is open by three steps.
  • Fareed series (2-4-1, 2-3-2, anything with two high): four verts, deep cross, play-action bomb, post-corner. Two-deep safeties are two guys who can't help on a crosser, so make them decide and then throw behind the decision.
  • Never tell the defense which one is in. Same formations, same motions, same cadence. Their whole coverage call changes when the QB changes, and you want them guessing wrong.
  • Throwback / orbit motion is the cheat code: Kai motions like he's going and Fareed is the throwback, or Fareed motions and Kai leaks. Both are live with either guy on the field.

3. Scramble drill — a rule, not a play

The moment the QB breaks contain or gets flushed, four things happen at once, every single time, no signal needed:

  1. Nearest eligible comes back to the QB at 8-10 yards. He is the bail-out.
  2. The receiver on the QB's throwing-hand side (his right, for a righty) takes his route deep — he is the bomb if the defense turns its back.
  3. The receiver on the opposite side crosses the field behind the QB's path. In flag this is a 30-yard play because everybody's chasing the QB.
  4. Everyone else gets to a window and stays put — don't drift into the QB's eyes. Sit down, hands up.

Run the scramble drill for 5 minutes every practice until it's reflex. On a team with a mobile QB this is worth more than any single play in the book — it turns a dead play into a first down.

4. Motion rules

  • Motion is a read, not decoration. Jet and orbit motion tell you coverage: if a defender follows across the formation, it's man. If nobody travels, it's zone. Call the answer off the motion, don't call the play and hope.
  • Motion before the snap is legal and free; use it to turn a 2x2 into a 3x1 and back so they never get a clean bead on your alignment.
  • Motion a man into a chip lane when the rusher is beating you. A body arriving on the move beats a body standing still.
  • Never motion a man across and then throw it back to him unless you've repped the throwback. If you have — it's a walk-in.

5. Coverage keys (what tells you man vs zone)

  • Eyes: man defenders look at you. Zone defenders look at the QB.
  • Depth: nobody deeper than 8 yards pre-snap with bodies tight = man/press. Bodies at 8-10 with heads on the QB = zone.
  • Motion: man travels, zone doesn't (usually — some zone teams travel and it's a lie, so motion twice).
  • Count the deep: 1 safety = throw the post behind the crosser. 2 safeties = throw the seam and the over, and run Kai at them.

If a defense is disguising well (walking a safety down late, bluffs), call the two-play: a quick game to the side they show, and a vertical tagged on it. Whichever way they jump, one of the two is right.

6. Cadence and snap count

  • Vary it. The rush can leave on the ball, so a hard count gets you a free 5 and gets him guessing.
  • On obvious passing downs, snap on 1 — don't let them time it.
  • In the red zone, snap on 1 every time. No thinking, no pre-snap penalties.

7. Five plays to install first

If you only get one practice: Bubble Now, Slant-Flat, Post-Wheel, Four Verts, QB Power. That is a complete offense. Everything else in this book is a variation on one of those five.
